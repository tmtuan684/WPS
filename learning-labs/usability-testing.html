Lecture · Foundations

What is usability testing?

Usability is about people — their understanding of something and their ability to use it. It is not, first and foremost, about the technology underneath. In the context of web app development, that idea narrows to something testable:

Usability is about users' understanding of a web application and their use of its functionality.
Usability testing evaluates whether users' understanding of a web application, and their use of its functionality, align with its intended purpose.

That gap between intended purpose and actual understanding is exactly what Part A of this week's exercise is designed to expose in your own forum.

The seven attributes of usability

When you judge whether a feature is "usable," you're really judging it against seven separate questions. A feature can pass some of these and fail others — a search bar can be effective but not delightful, or memorable but not efficient.

AttributeQuestion it answers
UsefulDoes the app do something users actually need done?
LearnableCan users easily figure out how to use a feature?
MemorableDo users have to relearn how to use a feature every time they return to it?
EffectiveDoes a feature fulfil a task that users need to complete?
EfficientDoes it fulfil that task within a reasonable amount of time and effort?
DesirableDo users actually want this specific feature or app?
DelightfulAre users satisfied — not just able to get by — while using it?

Three principles of web app usability

These principles come from Steve Krug's Don't Make Me Think and give you something concrete to test your own forum against, not just abstract qualities to hope for.

Principle 1 · "Don't make me think" — Krug's First Law of Usability
  • App structure should be self-evident, or at least self-explanatory
  • The app should be usable without a manual
  • Features should be easy to use, and controls should be consistent
  • Ambiguity should be reduced and removed wherever it appears
Apply it to your forum
  • Can users figure out how to search or filter threads in the current page design?
  • Can the navigation be used without instructions?
  • Does a feature like "reply" or "delete post" behave the way users expect it to?
Principle 2 · App structure is scanned, not read
  • Users glance at a page, scan for text that reaches their goal, and click — they rarely read the whole page
  • People use a web app to achieve a particular purpose; they explore it rather than learn it
  • Content needs to be well-organised, tidy, and concise
  • Main content and actionable items (buttons, links, forms) must stand out and be clearly recognisable
Apply it to your forum
  • Are key features of the forum identifiable at a glance?
  • Do buttons and links clearly communicate what they do?
  • How would a normal user's eye actually move through a thread page?
Principle 3 · Help users know where they are
  • Web apps often contain what feels like "endless" links and pages
  • Users get confused when they don't know where they are within that structure
  • Confused users leave — so the app must work to keep users oriented
Apply it to your forum
  • Is a breadcrumb or equivalent orientation cue actually useful to users?
  • Do users use it to navigate, or ignore it?
  • Is the information users need available to them at this exact point in the interface?
  • Where are users likely to want to go next from this page — and is that obvious?

Why this matters

Usability testing is how you stop guessing

By now your team has a concept, has iterated on it, and has a working prototype of your forum. It's tempting to treat that prototype as finished. It isn't — it's a hypothesis about how people will create threads, reply, and find what they're looking for. This week's exercise gives you a lightweight, repeatable process for testing that hypothesis against real users before you lock in version 1.0.

Part A below walks your team through eight steps — from defining what to test through to reporting what you found. Part B is protected studio time to act on it: either prepare for testing, or implement changes from a round you've already run.

Part A · User Testing Exercise60 minutes

Define your objectives

Decide what part of the forum you actually want to learn about — don't test everything at once. Good candidates:

  • Ease of navigating between boards and threads
  • Clarity of the thread-creation and reply flow
  • Whether filtering and search actually surface the right threads

Keep both the interface (does it look clear?) and the experience (does it feel obvious what to do next?) in view — they fail in different ways.

Write realistic test scenarios

Turn your objectives into concrete tasks a participant can actually attempt — not "explore the site," but specific actions:

  • Create a new thread with a given subject and opening post
  • Reply to an existing post inside a thread
  • Filter threads by a keyword or date range
  • Delete a post, and see how the thread view responds

Mix in at least one edge case or less-common action — that's often where the real problems live.

Recruit test participants

Trade with another team in the studio — their unfamiliarity with your forum is the whole point; a teammate who already knows the UI can't give you a first-time reaction.

  • Aim for a handful of different participants, not just one
  • Brief each participant on their role before you start, not what to click

Conduct the test

Sit the participant down with your scenarios and get out of the way as much as you can.

  • Ask them to think aloud as they work — narrating confusion is more useful than silence
  • Take notes on where they hesitate, backtrack, or misclick — not just whether they finish
  • Resist the urge to jump in and explain; if they're stuck, that's data

Collect feedback

Combine what you observed with what participants say directly, using both structured and open questions:

  • Structured: "How easy was it to create a new thread?" (e.g. 1–5 scale)
  • Open-ended: "What did you find confusing or frustrating?"

Keep the feedback form short — a form participants abandon halfway through gives you half a dataset.

Analyse the results

Look across all participants for patterns, not just individual complaints.

  • Group similar issues together — three people struggling with the same button is a signal, one person is a data point
  • Rank issues by how much they actually hurt the experience, not by how easy they are to fix

Implement changes

Fix what matters most first, and confirm the fix actually worked.

  • Address the highest-impact issues before minor polish
  • Re-check changed flows against the original scenarios where you can
  • Keep a short record of what you changed and why — you'll want this for your report

Report your findings

Write up the process for your own record and, later, your submission.

  • Objectives, scenarios, who you tested with, and how
  • Key findings and the changes you made in response
  • A short reflection: what surprised you, what would you test differently next time
Basic advice, restated
Clear objectives keep the session focused. Realistic scenarios surface real problems. Diverse participants catch more than a single perspective ever will. Mixed feedback methods (observation + structured + open questions) triangulate better than any one alone. And if you have time, a second short round of testing after your changes is worth more than polishing the first round's write-up.
Part B · Studio Project Work120 minutes

Protected time to act on it

By this point your team should have a concept, an innovation process behind you, and a working prototype. The job now is to test and verify that implementation before you lock in v1.0. Use this block in whichever way actually moves your team forward:

Pre-testing

  • Finish the draft your usability test will run against
  • Prepare scenarios, feedback forms, and a testing schedule with your partner team

Post-testing

  • Work through your findings and implement the changes you prioritised
  • Re-verify fixed flows against the original scenarios

Otherwise, use the time to catch up on any earlier workshop material your team hasn't finished. Staff are circulating for feedback and troubleshooting throughout.

Presentation checklist

Run through the full presentation once as a team to check timing and that it flows as one narrative, not four separate talks.
Every team member can demonstrate their part of the site and explain the reasoning behind it, not just click through it.
Slides are reviewed for clarity — each one earns its place, nothing is there to fill space.
Technical setup is checked in advance: site loads, audio/video works, you're not debugging live in front of the room.
What the presentation should cover

Tell the story of the project, not just the demo

  • A short introduction to your team
  • An overview of the forum and the problem it addresses
  • A walkthrough of key features and the UX decisions behind them
  • What your usability testing found, and what you changed because of it
